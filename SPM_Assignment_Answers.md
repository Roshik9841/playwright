### Q1. Define project and software project with examples.

**Definition of a Project**

A project is a planned activity involving non-routine tasks that require planning, specific objectives to be met, a pre-determined time span, and constrained resources. It is carried out for someone other than yourself, involves several specialisms, and is carried out in several phases.

*Example:* Building a bridge, constructing a hospital, or developing a new IT system.

**Characteristics of a Project:**
- Non-routine tasks are involved
- Planning is required
- Specific objectives are to be met or a specified product is to be created
- It has a pre-determined time span
- Work is carried out for someone other than yourself
- Resources available are constrained
- The project is large or complex

**Definition of a Software Project**

A software project is a collection and integration of various components of software. It begins with early investigation and ends with implementation. A complete life cycle of software development can be seen within it.

*Example:* Developing an online banking system, creating a hospital management system, or building an e-commerce platform.

### Q2. Differentiate between software projects and other engineering projects.

Software projects differ from traditional engineering projects due to the following unique characteristics:

**i. Invisibility**
When a physical artifact such as a bridge or road is being constructed, the progress being made can actually be seen. With software, progress is not immediately visible. There is no tangible product to examine during development.

**ii. Complexity**
Per dollar, pound, or euro spent, software products contain more complexity than other engineered artifacts. The internal logic, data structures, and interdependencies of software are far more complex than those of physical structures.

**iii. Conformity**
The traditional engineer is usually working with physical systems and physical materials like cement and steel. These systems can have some complexity but are governed by physical laws that are consistent. Software developers have to conform to the requirements of human clients, which certainly keeps changing and fluctuating.

**iv. Flexibility**
The ease with which software can be changed is usually seen as one of its strengths. However, software systems are also subject to a high degree of change — both during and after development — unlike physical structures that are generally rigid once built.

### Q5. Describe the software project management process.

The SPM process consists of the following phases:

**i. Feasibility Study**
An investigation into whether a prospective project is worth starting. It covers information gathering, cost estimation, benefit analysis, and strategic planning including technical, economic, legal, operational, and schedule feasibility.

**ii. Planning**
Once feasibility is proven, planning begins. For large projects, an outline is formulated first. Planning includes defining objectives, identifying activities, allocating resources, identifying risks, and setting milestones.

**iii. Project Execution**
The project is executed in two sub-phases:
- *Design:* Making decisions about the precise form of the products — user interface design, data flow design, and internal architecture design.
- *Implementation:* The real-time execution and building of the final software product.

**iv. Monitoring and Control**
Involves checking on progress (monitoring) and taking action to remedy holdups (controlling). The project control cycle involves data collection, data processing, decision-making, and implementation.

**v. Closedown**
The final phase where the project is formally completed, documentation is finalized, users are trained, and the system is handed over.

### Q1. Compare software project management with traditional project management in detail.

Software Project Management (SPM) and Traditional Project Management (TPM) share some common principles, but differ significantly in several key aspects:

**i. Nature of the Product**

*Traditional PM:* Deals with physical, tangible deliverables such as buildings, roads, bridges, or manufactured goods. Progress is directly visible and measurable.

*Software PM:* Deals with intangible products — software systems that cannot be physically seen or touched. Progress is largely invisible, making it harder to measure and communicate.

**ii. Complexity**

*Traditional PM:* Physical projects follow well-established engineering principles and natural laws (e.g., physics, chemistry). Complexity is relatively predictable.

*Software PM:* Per unit cost, software contains far greater complexity than physical artifacts. The complexity arises from logic, data interdependencies, and integration across systems.

**iii. Conformity / Requirement Changes**

*Traditional PM:* Requirements are relatively stable. A building's design doesn't drastically change once construction begins.

*Software PM:* Requirements frequently change during development due to evolving client needs. Software must continuously conform to these changing requirements.

**iv. Flexibility / Change Management**

*Traditional PM:* Once physical structures are built, changes are costly and difficult.

*Software PM:* Software can be easily modified, which is a strength but also leads to uncontrolled scope creep if not managed carefully.

**v. Estimation and Planning**

*Traditional PM:* Historical data and well-established formulas (e.g., for civil engineering) make estimation more reliable.

*Software PM:* Estimation is notoriously difficult due to the uniqueness of each project, invisibility of progress, and dependency on human skill.

**vi. Team Skills**

*Traditional PM:* Requires physical labor alongside technical skills (engineering, architecture).

*Software PM:* Requires highly specialized cognitive skills — programming, system design, database management, etc. — making team management uniquely challenging.

**vii. Testing and Quality**

*Traditional PM:* Quality can often be assessed by physical inspection.

*Software PM:* Quality requires rigorous functional and non-functional testing, code reviews, and user acceptance testing.

**viii. Risk**

*Traditional PM:* Risks often relate to physical hazards (weather, material failure).

*Software PM:* Risks relate to changing requirements, technology failures, personnel turnover, underestimation, and integration challenges.

**Summary Table:**

| Aspect | Traditional PM | Software PM |
|---|---|---|
| Product | Physical & tangible | Intangible (software) |
| Visibility | Directly observable | Largely invisible |
| Requirements | Relatively stable | Frequently changing |
| Estimation | More reliable | Difficult & uncertain |
| Complexity | Governed by physical laws | Highly complex logic |
| Change | Costly & difficult | Easy but risky |
| Quality Check | Physical inspection | Testing & verification |

### Q2. Explain the complete process of Software Project Management with a suitable diagram.

The Software Project Management (SPM) process follows a structured lifecycle:

**Phase 1: Feasibility Study**
The first step determines whether the project is worth pursuing. It covers:
- *Technical Feasibility:* Is the technology available and capable?
- *Economic Feasibility:* Do the benefits outweigh the costs?
- *Legal Feasibility:* Are there legal or regulatory constraints?
- *Operational Feasibility:* Can the system be used as intended?
- *Schedule Feasibility:* Can the project be completed in the required time?

**Phase 2: Project Planning**
Once feasibility is confirmed, detailed planning begins:
- Establish project constraints (time, cost, scope)
- Make initial estimates of project parameters
- Define milestones and deliverables
- Draw up the project schedule
- Identify and plan for risks

**Phase 3: Project Execution**
The project is carried out in two sub-phases:
- *Design Phase:* Creating user interface designs, database designs, and system architecture.
- *Implementation Phase:* Actual coding, integration, and building of the software.

**Phase 4: Monitoring and Control**
- Collecting data on actual progress
- Comparing actual performance against the plan
- Taking corrective action when deviations occur
- Updating schedules and re-estimating as needed

**Phase 5: Closedown**
- System testing and user acceptance testing
- Handing over the completed system to the client
- Training users
- Preparing final documentation
- Reviewing lessons learned

**The Project Lifecycle:**
```
Requirement Analysis → Specification → Design → Coding
→ Verification & Validation → Implementation/Installation → Maintenance
```

**The Control Cycle:**
```
Define Objectives → Data Collection → Data Processing → Modelling
→ Making Decisions/Plans → Implementation → (back to real world)
```

### Q3. Discuss the major challenges faced by software project managers and suggest possible solutions.

**Challenge 1: Poor Estimates and Plans**

*Problem:* Inaccurate time and cost estimates lead to project overruns.

*Solution:* Use structured estimation techniques such as COCOMO, expert judgment, or analogy-based estimation. Use historical data from past projects to improve accuracy.

**Challenge 2: Changing Requirements**

*Problem:* Clients frequently change requirements during development, leading to scope creep.

*Solution:* Implement a formal change management process. Use agile methodologies to accommodate change in a controlled manner.

**Challenge 3: Lack of Skilled Resources**

*Problem:* Finding and retaining skilled personnel is difficult in the competitive software industry.

*Solution:* Invest in training and development. Plan for resource needs early. Build cross-functional team skills.

**Challenge 4: Communication Gaps**

*Problem:* Poor communication between clients, users, and developers leads to misunderstandings and rework.

*Solution:* Establish regular status meetings, use collaboration tools, and assign a clear communication plan with defined channels and responsibilities.

**Challenge 5: Risk Management Failures**

*Problem:* Unforeseen risks derail projects.

*Solution:* Conduct proactive risk identification and maintain a risk register. Develop contingency plans for high-priority risks.

**Challenge 6: Invisible Progress**

*Problem:* Unlike physical construction, software progress cannot be directly observed.

*Solution:* Use measurable milestones, progress reports, burndown charts, and regular code reviews to make progress visible.

**Challenge 7: Team Conflicts**

*Problem:* Conflicts among team members reduce productivity.

*Solution:* The project manager must develop strong conflict resolution skills and foster a collaborative team culture.

### Q9. Discuss the relationship between project scope, time, cost, and quality in software projects.

The relationship between scope, time, cost, and quality is often called the **"Project Management Triangle"** or **"Iron Triangle"**.

**i. Scope**
Scope defines what the project will deliver. Expanding scope without adjusting time or cost leads to project failure.

**ii. Time**
Time is the schedule within which the project must be completed. Compressing time usually requires additional cost or a reduction in scope.

**iii. Cost**
Cost covers all the financial resources required. Cutting costs usually impacts quality or requires scope reduction.

**iv. Quality**
Quality is the standard to which the product must conform. Maintaining quality while under time and cost pressure is the fundamental challenge of project management.

**Key Relationships:**

- **Scope ↑ → Time ↑ and/or Cost ↑:** More features require more time and money.
- **Time ↓ → Cost ↑ or Quality ↓:** Compressing the schedule requires more resources (crash cost) or trade-offs in quality.
- **Cost ↓ → Scope ↓ or Quality ↓:** Budget cuts force reduction of features or lower quality.
- **Quality ↑ → Time ↑ and Cost ↑:** Higher quality standards require more rigorous testing and rework.

**Implication for Project Managers:**
A project manager must constantly balance these four dimensions. If a client requests additional features (scope), the PM must renegotiate either time, cost, or quality to maintain balance. This is often expressed as: *"Good, fast, or cheap — pick two."*

### Q1. Define software project scheduling.

Software project scheduling is concerned with the techniques that can be employed to manage the activities that need to be undertaken during the development of a project. It involves separating the total work involved in a project into separate activities and organizing the work so that the workforce is optimally utilized.

A software project manager doing scheduling must:
- Identify all the tasks needed to complete the project
- Break down large tasks into smaller activities
- Determine the dependency among different activities
- Establish the most likely time estimates for completing each activity
- Allocate resources to activities
- Plan starting and ending dates for various activities
- Determine the critical path

---

### Q2. Explain the objectives of activity planning.

The objectives of activity planning in software project scheduling are:

**i. Feasibility Assessment**
To determine whether the proposed project timescales and resource requirements are achievable within the constraints.

**ii. Resource Allocation**
To identify what resources (staff, hardware, software) are needed and when they are needed, enabling optimal allocation.

**iii. Detailed Costing**
Once resources are allocated to activities, the cost of each activity — and the project as a whole — can be calculated.

**iv. Motivation**
Clearly defined targets and milestones provide the team with goals to work towards, motivating performance.

**v. Coordination**
Activity planning coordinates the work of different team members by making clear the dependencies between tasks and who is responsible for what.

**vi. Basis for Control**
A detailed activity plan provides a baseline against which actual progress can be measured, making it possible to identify and correct deviations.

### Q3. What is Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)?

WBS is a technique used to decompose a given task into smaller, manageable activities. It provides a notation for representing the major tasks needed to carry out in order to solve a problem.

**Key Features of WBS:**
- The root of the tree is labelled by the problem/project name.
- Each node of the tree is broken down into smaller activities, which become children of that node.
- Each activity is decomposed into smaller sub-activities until the leaf level, where activities require approximately two weeks to develop.
- The result is a hierarchical tree structure representing all the work required.

**Example:**
For a Management Information System (MIS) software:
- Root: MIS Software
  - Database Module
    - Design Database Schema
    - Create Tables
    - Write Stored Procedures
  - User Interface Module
    - Design Screens
    - Implement Forms
  - Reporting Module
    - Generate Reports
    - Export to PDF

**ii. Better Estimation**
By breaking the project into small tasks, each task can be estimated more accurately, leading to better overall estimates.

**iii. Accountability**
Each work package can be assigned to a specific team member, creating clear accountability.

By examining each component individually, potential risks at the task level can be identified early.

### Q5. Define Critical Path Method (CPM).

The Critical Path Method (CPM) is a project scheduling technique that identifies the sequence of activities that determines the minimum duration needed to complete the project.

**Key Concepts:**

- **Activity Network:** WBS is transformed into an activity network showing activities, their estimated durations, and inter-dependencies.
- **Critical Path:** The longest path through the network of activities. Any delay in an activity on the critical path will directly delay the project completion.
- **Slack Time:** The amount of time an activity can be delayed without delaying the project. Activities on the critical path have zero slack.

**Steps in CPM:**
1. List all activities and their durations.
2. Identify dependencies between activities.
3. Draw the activity network.
4. Perform forward pass to find earliest start/finish times.
5. Perform backward pass to find latest start/finish times.
6. Calculate slack for each activity.
7. Identify the critical path (activities with zero slack).

**Origin:** CPM originated from industry (as opposed to PERT, which originated from the military).

---

### Q6. Explain Program Evaluation and Review Technique (PERT).

PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique) is a project scheduling technique that uses probabilistic time estimates to account for uncertainty in task durations.

**Key Features:**

**i. Three Time Estimates**
For each activity, three estimates are taken:
- **Optimistic time (o/a):** The shortest time if everything goes perfectly.
- **Most likely time (m/r):** The time expected under normal circumstances.
- **Pessimistic time (p/b):** The worst possible time accounting for all reasonable eventualities.

**ii. Expected Time Formula**
```
ET = (o + 4m + p) / 6
```
or
```
te = (a + 4m + b) / 6
```

**iii. Standard Deviation**
PERT also calculates the standard deviation to measure uncertainty:
```
s = (b – a) / 6
```

**iv. Probabilistic Nature**
Because PERT accounts for uncertainty, it is probabilistic in nature, unlike CPM which is deterministic.

**Applications:**
PERT is useful for monitoring timely progress and identifying all parallel activities in a project. It is particularly useful when activity durations are uncertain.

---

### Q7. Differentiate between CPM and PERT.

| Feature | CPM | PERT |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Industry | Military |
| Time Estimate | Single estimate | Three estimates (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic) |
| Nature | Deterministic | Probabilistic |
| Uncertainty | Does not consider time uncertainty | Considers time uncertainty |
| Average Time | Does not use average time | Uses weighted average time (ET formula) |
| Focus | Cost-based | Time-delay based |
| Use Case | Projects with well-known durations | Projects with uncertain durations |

**CPM** is best when activity durations are known from past experience, while **PERT** is preferred for research and development or new types of projects where durations are uncertain.

---

### Q8. What is Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM)?

The Precedence Diagramming Method (PDM), also known as the Activity on Node (AON) method, is a graphical representation technique that shows the interdependencies of activities and is used in schedule development.

**Key Features:**
- Represented by rectangles (nodes) for activities or milestones.
- Arrows connect nodes to show relationships and duration.
- Also known as Activity on Node (AON) diagrams.

**Four Dependency Relationships in PDM:**

**i. Finish to Start (FS):** The next activity cannot start until the first is complete. (Most common relationship.)
*Example:* You must build a wall before painting it.

**ii. Finish to Finish (FF):** The next activity cannot finish until the first is finished.
*Example:* Coding cannot finish until all requirements are received from the client.

**iii. Start to Start (SS):** The next activity cannot start until the first starts. Both activities start simultaneously.
*Example:* Wall cleaning and wall coating can start at the same time with different teams.

**iv. Start to Finish (SF):** The next activity cannot finish until the first starts.
*Example:* Moving into a new home cannot finish until the new home construction starts.

---

### Q9. Explain forward pass and backward pass calculations.

Forward pass and backward pass are calculations used in CPM/PERT to determine the earliest and latest times for each activity.

**Forward Pass (Earliest Times)**

The forward pass calculates:
- **Earliest Start (ES):** The earliest time an activity can begin.
- **Earliest Finish (EF):** ES + Duration.

Process: Start from the first activity and move forward. For each activity, ES = the latest EF of all its predecessors.

**Backward Pass (Latest Times)**

The backward pass calculates:
- **Latest Finish (LF):** The latest time an activity can finish without delaying the project.
- **Latest Start (LS):** LF – Duration.

Process: Start from the last activity (set LF = project completion time) and move backward. For each activity, LF = the earliest LS of all its successors.

**Slack Time Calculation:**
```
Slack = LS – ES = LF – EF
```

Activities with Slack = 0 are on the **Critical Path**.

---

### Q10. Define critical activities in project scheduling.

Critical activities are those activities in a project schedule that have zero slack time, meaning they cannot be delayed without delaying the overall project completion date.

**Key Characteristics of Critical Activities:**
- They lie on the critical path — the longest path through the network.
- Any delay in a critical activity directly delays the project.
- They require the closest monitoring and attention from the project manager.
- Resources must be prioritized for critical activities.

**Identifying Critical Activities:**
1. Perform forward pass to calculate earliest completion times.
2. Perform backward pass to calculate latest completion times.
3. Calculate slack for each activity (Slack = LS – ES).
4. Activities with Slack = 0 are critical activities.

**Importance:**
The project manager must focus special attention on critical activities. Delay prevention strategies (crashing, fast-tracking) should be applied to critical activities when the project is running behind schedule.

### Q2. Discuss the Critical Path Method with a suitable numerical example.

**Definition:**
CPM is a scheduling technique used to identify the longest path of planned activities to the end of the project, and the earliest and latest start and finish dates for each activity.

**Steps in CPM:**

1. List all activities with durations.
2. Identify dependencies.
3. Draw the activity network.
4. Perform forward pass (calculate ES and EF).
5. Perform backward pass (calculate LS and LF).
6. Calculate slack.
7. Identify critical path (zero slack activities).

**Numerical Example:**

Consider a software project with the following activities:

| Activity | Duration (days) | Predecessors |
|---|---|---|
| A – Requirements | 5 | None |
| B – Design | 7 | A |
| C – Database Setup | 3 | A |
| D – Coding | 10 | B |
| E – Testing | 6 | C, D |
| F – Deployment | 2 | E |

**Forward Pass:**
- A: ES=0, EF=5
- B: ES=5, EF=12
- C: ES=5, EF=8
- D: ES=12, EF=22
- E: ES=max(8,22)=22, EF=28
- F: ES=28, EF=30

**Project Duration = 30 days**

**Backward Pass:**
- F: LF=30, LS=28
- E: LF=28, LS=22
- D: LF=22, LS=12
- C: LF=22, LS=19
- B: LF=12, LS=5
- A: LF=min(5,19)=5, LS=0

**Slack Calculation:**

| Activity | ES | EF | LS | LF | Slack |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 5 | 0 | 5 | 0 ← Critical |
| B | 5 | 12 | 5 | 12 | 0 ← Critical |
| C | 5 | 8 | 19 | 22 | 14 |
| D | 12 | 22 | 12 | 22 | 0 ← Critical |
| E | 22 | 28 | 22 | 28 | 0 ← Critical |
| F | 28 | 30 | 28 | 30 | 0 ← Critical |

**Critical Path: A → B → D → E → F (30 days)**

Activity C has 14 days of slack and is not critical.

### Q3. Explain PERT technique and illustrate its application in software project scheduling.

**Definition:**
PERT (Program Evaluation and Review Technique) is a probabilistic scheduling technique that uses three time estimates to account for uncertainty in activity durations.

**Three Time Estimates:**
- **Optimistic (a/o):** Best case scenario time.
- **Most Likely (m/r):** Time under normal circumstances.
- **Pessimistic (b/p):** Worst case scenario time.

**Expected Time Formula:**
```
te = (a + 4m + b) / 6
```

**Standard Deviation Formula:**
```
s = (b – a) / 6
```

**Application Example:**

Consider a software project with the following PERT estimates:

| Activity | Optimistic (a) | Most Likely (m) | Pessimistic (b) | te = (a+4m+b)/6 | s = (b-a)/6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A – Requirements | 3 | 5 | 9 | 5.33 | 1.00 |
| B – Design | 5 | 7 | 12 | 7.50 | 1.17 |
| C – Coding | 8 | 12 | 20 | 12.67 | 2.00 |
| D – Testing | 3 | 5 | 10 | 5.50 | 1.17 |
| E – Deployment | 1 | 2 | 4 | 2.17 | 0.50 |

**Interpretation:**
- Activity C has the highest standard deviation (2.00), meaning it carries the highest scheduling risk.
- The expected project duration can be calculated using the critical path through these expected times.
- PERT allows the project manager to calculate the probability of completing the project by a specific date.

**Advantages of PERT:**
- Accounts for uncertainty in time estimates.
- Identifies high-risk activities (those with high standard deviation).
- Enables probability calculations for project completion dates.

|---|---|---|---|

| Best For | Well-defined projects | Research/uncertain projects | Projects with complex dependencies |

### Q5. Explain the process of identifying critical activities in a software project.

**Critical activities** are those with zero slack time — any delay in these activities will delay the project.

**Step-by-Step Process:**

**Step 1: List All Activities and Durations**
Create a complete list of all project activities along with their estimated durations.

**Step 2: Identify Dependencies**
Determine which activities must be completed before others can begin (precedence relationships).

**Step 3: Draw the Activity Network**
Represent activities as nodes and dependencies as arrows in a network diagram.

**Step 4: Perform Forward Pass**
Starting from the first activity:
- ES (Earliest Start) of first activity = 0
- EF (Earliest Finish) = ES + Duration
- For subsequent activities: ES = maximum EF of all predecessors

**Step 5: Perform Backward Pass**
Starting from the last activity:
- LF (Latest Finish) of last activity = Project Duration
- LS (Latest Start) = LF – Duration
- For preceding activities: LF = minimum LS of all successors

**Step 6: Calculate Slack**
```
Slack = LS – ES (or LF – EF)
```

**Step 7: Identify Critical Path**
Activities with Slack = 0 form the critical path and are the critical activities.

**Importance of Identifying Critical Activities:**
- They must be monitored most closely.
- Resources should be prioritized for critical activities.
- Any delay in a critical activity must trigger immediate corrective action.
- Schedule crashing (adding resources) should focus on critical activities.

# CHAPTER 3: Software Estimation Techniques

## PART A: 5 Marks Questions

---

### Q1. Define software effort estimation.

Software effort estimation is the process of calculating the approximate amount of time, cost, and resources required to develop a software product. It is a rough calculation that helps project managers plan and budget effectively.

Effort estimation helps in:
- Providing forecasts to clients
- Budgeting funds and resources for project success
- Planning project schedules
- Assessing feasibility of proposed projects

The six key areas that benefit from estimation techniques are: **cost, time, scope, risk, resources, and quality**.

---

### Q2. Explain the problems caused by overestimation in software projects.

Overestimation occurs when more time or resources are estimated than actually needed. The problems it causes can be explained through:

**i. Parkinson's Law**
*"Work expands to fill the time available."*
If a task is given more time than needed, developers tend to slow their pace to fill that time, reducing efficiency.

**ii. Brook's Law**
*"Putting more people on a late job makes it later."*
If a project is overestimated in effort, more staff may be allocated than are needed. This leads to increased management overhead, coordination problems, and communication delays — making the project take longer.

**Other Problems:**
- Unnecessary resource allocation leading to higher costs.
- Missed market opportunities due to delayed releases.
- Reduced sense of urgency, leading to lower team productivity.
- Poor organizational credibility if overestimates are regularly submitted.

---

### Q3. Explain the problems caused by underestimation in software projects.

Underestimation occurs when less time or resources are allocated than actually required:

**i. Schedule Overruns**
The project takes longer than planned, causing missed deadlines and client dissatisfaction.

**ii. Budget Overruns**
Additional resources must be brought in urgently, often at premium cost, exceeding the planned budget.

**iii. Quality Degradation**
Under pressure to meet unrealistic deadlines, developers cut corners on testing and quality, leading to defect-ridden software.

**iv. Team Burnout**
Developers are forced to work overtime to meet unrealistic targets, leading to stress, burnout, and eventual staff turnover.

**v. Client Relationship Damage**
Missed commitments damage trust with the client and may lead to contractual penalties or project cancellation.

**vi. Cascading Delays**
Delays in one activity cause delays in all dependent activities, rippling through the entire schedule.

---

### Q4. What are the bases of software estimation?

Software estimation is based on the following key foundations:

**i. Historical Data**
Nearly all estimation methods need information about how similar projects were implemented in the past. This data must be carefully filtered for relevance, accounting for differences in programming languages, tools, standards, and staff experience.

**ii. Measure of Work (Size Metrics)**
The work content of the system is expressed independently of effort, using measures such as:
- **SLOC (Source Lines of Code):** Count of lines of code to be written.
- **KLOC:** Thousands of lines of code.
- **Function Points:** Measure based on functional requirements.

**iii. Complexity**
Two programs with the same KLOC will not take the same time if one is more complex. Complexity adjustments must be applied to raw size estimates, often using subjective judgment or complexity scoring models.

**iv. Environmental Factors**
Programming language, tools available, development standards, team experience, and organizational factors all influence effort.

**v. Estimation Technique**
The choice of technique (expert judgment, analogy, parametric model) also affects the basis and accuracy of estimates.

---

### Q5. Define expert judgment estimation technique.

Expert judgment is an estimation technique that involves consulting with a software cost estimation expert or group of experts who use their experience and understanding of the proposed project to arrive at an estimate of its effort.

**Key Characteristics:**

- Relies on the estimator's knowledge of the application area or development environment.
- Most appropriate when estimating the effort needed to change an existing piece of software.
- Requires impact analysis — the estimator judges what proportion of code will be affected and derives an estimate from that.
- Someone already familiar with the software is in the best position to provide this estimate.

**Advantages:**
- Quick and low-cost method of estimation.
- Captures tacit knowledge that may not be captured in formal models.
- Useful for novel or unusual projects.

**Disadvantages:**
- Subject to bias and overconfidence.
- Results may vary widely between different experts.
- Difficult to audit or justify to stakeholders.

---

### Q6. Explain estimation by analogy method.

Estimation by analogy, also known as **case-based reasoning**, involves identifying completed projects that are similar to the new project and using their actual effort as the basis for estimating the new project.

**Process:**

**Step 1:** Search for completed projects (source cases) with characteristics similar to the new project (target case).

**Step 2:** Use the recorded effort of the matching source case as a base estimate for the target.

**Step 3:** Identify differences between the source and target projects and make adjustments to the base estimate accordingly.

**When to Use:**
- When information about some previous projects is available but not enough to build generalized parametric models.
- When the new project is similar to a well-understood past project.

**Advantages:**
- Grounded in real historical data.
- Relatively simple to apply.
- Intuitive and easy to explain to stakeholders.

**Disadvantages:**
- Requires good historical data from comparable projects.
- Difficult to identify truly comparable projects.
- Adjustments for differences are often subjective.

---

### Q7. What is Bottom-up estimation?

Bottom-up estimation is an approach where the project is broken down into its smallest component tasks, each task is estimated individually, and these estimates are then aggregated (summed) to get the overall project estimate.

**Process:**
1. Break the project into tasks using WBS.
2. Break each task into sub-tasks until individual tasks can be estimated by a single person.
3. Estimate the effort for each smallest task.
4. Aggregate all task estimates upward to get the total project estimate.

**When to Use:**
- At detailed stages of project planning.
- When the project is novel and no historical data is available.
- When accurate, detailed estimates are required.

**Advantages:**
- More accurate for well-defined projects.
- Promotes thorough understanding of project scope.
- Provides task-level accountability.

**Disadvantages:**
- Time-consuming to produce.
- Requires detailed knowledge of all tasks upfront.
- May miss integration and overhead efforts if not carefully considered.

---

### Q8. Define Top-down estimation approach.

Top-down estimation is an approach where an overall estimate is formulated for the entire project first, and is then broken down into the effort required for individual component tasks.

**Key Characteristics:**
- Normally associated with **parametric (algorithmic) models** such as COCOMO.
- Uses mathematical equations based on research and historical data.
- Inputs include: Lines of Code (LOC), number of functions, programming language, design methodology, skill levels, and risk assessments.

**Process:**
1. Estimate the overall project size (e.g., KLOC or function points).
2. Apply a parametric model to derive total effort.
3. Distribute the total effort across phases and components.

**Advantages:**
- Quick to produce.
- Provides a global view of the project.
- Useful in early project phases when details are not yet known.

**Disadvantages:**
- May lack detail at the component level.
- Relies on the accuracy of the parametric model.
- Can miss specific technical challenges within individual modules.

---

### Q9. Explain parametric estimation models.

Parametric estimation models (also called algorithmic models) use mathematical equations to estimate software development effort. These equations are based on empirical research and historical data from past software projects.

**General Formula:**
```
Effort = a × (Size)^b
```
Where `a` and `b` are constants derived from historical data, and Size is measured in KLOC or function points.

**Most Well-Known Model: COCOMO (Constructive Cost Model)**
Developed by Dr. Barry Boehm in 1981, based on data from 63 software projects.

**Three Project Modes in COCOMO:**

**i. Organic Mode:** Small teams, familiar environment, simple applications.
```
E = 2.4 × (KLOC)^1.05
```

**ii. Semi-detached Mode:** Mixed experience team, moderate complexity.
```
E = 3.0 × (KLOC)^1.12
```

**iii. Embedded Mode:** Complex hardware constraints, real-time systems.
```
E = 3.6 × (KLOC)^1.20
```

**Three Levels of COCOMO:**
- **Basic:** Uses only size and mode.
- **Intermediate:** Adds 15 cost drivers (personnel, product, project factors).
- **Detailed:** Phase-sensitive cost drivers and three-level product hierarchy.

---

### Q10. Compare Bottom-up and Top-down estimation methods.

| Feature | Bottom-up | Top-down |
|---|---|---|
| Starting Point | Smallest task components | Overall project size |
| Process | Estimate tasks, then aggregate | Estimate whole, then decompose |
| Associated Methods | WBS-based | Parametric models (COCOMO) |
| Accuracy | High (for well-defined projects) | Moderate (depends on model) |
| Time to Produce | Time-consuming | Quick |
| Best Used When | Detailed planning stage | Early planning stage |
| Risk | May miss integration costs | May miss component-level details |
| Knowledge Required | Detailed task knowledge | Historical data and formulas |

### Q1. Define project evaluation.

Project evaluation is the process of assessing whether a proposed project should be initiated. It involves comparing the proposed project against alternatives and deciding whether to proceed based on strategic, technical, and economic criteria.

Project evaluation is typically carried out as part of strategic planning or a feasibility study. It answers three key questions:
- Is this project aligned with organizational goals?
- Is it technically achievable?
- Is it economically worthwhile?

The risks involved also need to be evaluated as part of the project evaluation process.

---

### Q2. Explain strategic assessment in software projects.

Strategic assessment is used to determine whether a project fits within the long-term goals and direction of the organization.

**Key Characteristics:**
- Usually carried out by senior management.
- Requires a strategic plan that clearly defines the organizational objectives.
- Evaluates individual projects against the strategic plan.

**Two types of strategic assessment:**

**i. Programme Management**
Individual projects are viewed as components of a larger organizational programme. The question is whether the project contributes to the long-term organizational goal and how it relates to other projects.

**ii. Portfolio Management**
For software companies developing products for clients, portfolio management assesses whether a new project fits within the company's portfolio in terms of resource competition, specialization, and diversification.

**Key Issues in Strategic Assessment:**
- Does the project align with long-term organizational goals?
- Will it increase market share?
- Does it fit the overall IS plan?
- How does it affect organizational structure?
- What are the staff implications?

---

### Q3. What is technical assessment?

Technical assessment evaluates whether a proposed system can be built using available technology and within organizational constraints.

It consists of evaluating the required functionality against:
- Available hardware and software
- Existing infrastructure
- Technical skills of the team

**Key Considerations:**
- Where an organization has a strategic IS plan, it places limitations on the types of solutions that may be considered.
- Technical constraints influence the cost of the solution and must be incorporated into the cost-benefit analysis.
- The technical assessment determines whether the proposed technology is mature enough, compatible with existing systems, and supportable by the organization's staff.

---

### Q4. Define cost-benefit analysis.

Cost-Benefit Analysis (CBA) is the most common method of carrying out an economic assessment of a proposed information system. It compares the expected costs of developing and operating the system with the benefits expected from having it in place.

**CBA answers three key questions:**
- Is the project worthwhile financially?
- Is it the best option among alternatives?
- Should it be undertaken at all?

**Two Steps of CBA:**
1. **Identify and estimate** all costs (development, setup, operational) and benefits (direct, indirect, intangible).
2. **Express all costs and benefits in common monetary units** to calculate the net benefit (total benefit minus total cost).

---

### Q5. Explain cash flow forecasting.

Cash flow forecasting is the process of predicting when expenditures and incomes will take place during a project's life cycle.

**Key Points:**

**i. Purpose**
It is vital to know when salaries, licensing fees, and other costs must be paid, and when payments from clients will be received.

**ii. Typical Cash Flow Pattern**
Products typically generate a negative cash flow during development (expenditures exceed income) followed by a positive cash flow during operation. There may also be decommissioning costs at the end of a product's life.

**iii. Challenges**
Accurate cash flow forecasting is difficult as it must be done early in the project's life cycle when many details are uncertain. It is conventional to ignore inflation effects to avoid adding further uncertainty.

**iv. Importance**
Without accurate cash flow forecasting, organizations can face liquidity crises even on profitable projects if income arrives much later than expenditure.

---

### Q6. What is risk evaluation in software projects?

Risk evaluation is the process of assessing the importance and potential impact of identified risks on a software project.

**Key Concepts:**

**i. Risk Likelihood**
The probability that a particular hazard will occur.

**ii. Risk Impact**
The effect the problem will have on the project if the hazard occurs.

**iii. Risk Exposure (Risk Value)**
Calculated as:
```
Risk Exposure = Risk Likelihood × Risk Impact
```

**iv. Risk Matrix**
A common approach is to construct a risk matrix that classifies each risk according to its likelihood and importance. This helps prioritize which risks need the most attention.

**v. Risk and NPV**
For risky projects, a higher discount rate (risk premium) is applied when calculating NPV to reflect the additional uncertainty. For example, 2% extra for a reasonably safe project or 5% for a fairly risky one.

---

### Q7. Explain the importance of selecting appropriate reports in project evaluation.

Selecting appropriate reports ensures that project evaluation provides actionable, relevant information to decision-makers.

**i. Informed Decision Making**
The right reports give senior management the information needed to decide whether to proceed with a project.

**ii. Cost-Benefit Visibility**
Financial reports (NPV, ROI, Payback Period) clearly show whether the project is economically viable.

**iii. Risk Visibility**
Risk assessment reports highlight potential threats and their management strategies.

**iv. Progress Tracking**
During execution, appropriate progress reports ensure that deviations from plan are caught early.

**v. Stakeholder Communication**

### Q10. Define schedule risk and its evaluation.

Schedule risk is the risk that the project will not be completed within its planned timeframe due to uncertainties in activity durations, resource availability, or unexpected events.

**Evaluation Methods:**

**i. PERT Analysis**
Using three time estimates (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic) and the formula:
```
te = (a + 4m + b) / 6
```
This quantifies uncertainty in activity durations and allows probability calculations for project completion dates.

**ii. Standard Deviation**
```
s = (b – a) / 6
```
A high standard deviation for an activity indicates high schedule risk for that activity.

**iii. Risk Matrix**
Classifying schedule risks by likelihood and impact to prioritize mitigation.

**iv. Critical Path Analysis**
Any risk affecting a critical path activity directly threatens the project's completion date.

**v. Risk Premium in NPV**
Higher discount rates are applied to account for schedule risk when evaluating project financial viability.

### Q2. Explain cost-benefit analysis techniques with examples.

Cost-Benefit Analysis involves comparing the costs and benefits of a project. The key evaluation techniques are:

**i. Net Profit (NP)**
The difference between total benefits and total costs over the life of the project.
```
Net Profit = Total Income – Total Cost
```
*Example:* A project costs Rs. 100,000 and generates Rs. 150,000 over 5 years: Net Profit = Rs. 50,000.

*Limitation:* Ignores timing of cash flows; Rs. 50,000 arriving in Year 5 is treated the same as Rs. 50,000 arriving in Year 1.

**ii. Payback Period (PBP)**
The time taken for cumulative income to equal the initial investment.

*Example:* A project with Rs. 100,000 investment and Rs. 25,000 annual income has a payback period of 4 years.

*Limitation:* Ignores overall profitability beyond the break-even point.

**iii. Return on Investment (ROI) / Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)**
```
ROI = (Average Annual Profit / Total Investment) × 100
```
*Example:* Net Profit = Rs. 50,000 over 5 years; Total Investment = Rs. 100,000.
Average Annual Profit = Rs. 10,000.
ROI = (10,000 / 100,000) × 100 = **10%**

*Limitation:* Ignores timing of cash flows and does not account for compound interest.

**iv. Net Present Value (NPV)**
Discounts future cash flows to their present value to account for the time value of money.
```
Present Value = Value in Year T / (1 + r)^T
NPV = Sum of all discounted cash flows
```
Where r = discount rate, T = year.

*Advantage:* Most accurate measure as it accounts for profitability AND timing.
*Limitation:* Choosing the appropriate discount rate is difficult.

**Decision Rule:** Accept the project if NPV > 0.

**v. Internal Rate of Return (IRR)**
The discount rate that makes NPV = 0. A project is worthwhile if its IRR exceeds the cost of capital or alternative investment returns.

### Q1. Define risk management in software projects.

Risk management is the process of identifying, assessing, and prioritizing risks to minimize, monitor, and control the probability of unfortunate or unforeseen events that could affect the successful completion of a software project.

**Objective:**
To avoid or minimize the adverse effects of unforeseen events by either avoiding the risks entirely or drawing up contingency plans to deal with them if they occur.

**Three Types of Risk:**
- **Estimation errors:** Caused by inherent difficulties of estimation.
- **Planning assumptions:** Due to assumptions made during the planning process that turn out to be incorrect.
- **Eventualities:** Unforeseen events occurring that were not planned for.

---

### Q2. Explain risk identification process.

Risk identification is the first step in risk management. Its objective is to list all risks that can adversely affect the successful execution of the project.

**Two Methods of Risk Identification:**

**i. Risk Analysis Scenario (Brainstorming)**
The risk team uses brainstorming based on their experience, judgment, and application domain knowledge to identify potential hazards.

**ii. Risk Checklist**
Pre-prepared lists of risks commonly found in software development projects. Project managers may use checklists specific to their domain or organization.

**Categories of Factors Considered During Risk Identification:**
- **Application factors:** Nature and size of the application.
- **Staff factors:** Experience, skill levels, staff satisfaction, and turnover rates.
- **Project factors:** Clarity of objectives, quality plan adherence.
- **Project methods:** Use of structured development methodologies.
- **Hardware/Software factors:** Familiarity with development platform.
- **Changeover factors:** All-at-once vs. incremental system changeover.
- **Supplier factors:** Dependency on external organizations.
- **Environment factors:** Changes in regulations, taxation, etc.
- **Health and safety factors:** Effects on participants and the environment.

---

### Q3. What is risk planning?

Risk planning is the process of identifying strategies to deal with the risks that have been identified during risk identification and evaluation.

**Risk Planning Methods:**

**i. Risk Acceptance**
A do-nothing approach for risks with the lowest priority. These are acceptable risks where the cost of mitigation exceeds the potential loss.

**ii. Risk Avoidance**
Avoiding activities or decisions that introduce high risk. For example, not using an unproven technology reduces the risk of technical failure.

**iii. Risk Contingency Plans / Risk Mitigation**
A plan that deals with a risk only if it actually occurs. For example, having a cross-trained backup developer in case the primary developer is ill.

**iv. Risk Resolution**
The execution of contingency plans when a risk occurs. The project manager responds, executes the action plan, and monitors progress.

**v. Risk Transfer**
Transferring the risk to another party. For example, outsourcing a risky component to a specialized external agency.

---

### Q4. Define risk evaluation.

Risk evaluation is the process of assessing the importance of identified risks to determine which risks require the most attention and resources.

**Key Concepts:**

**i. Risk Likelihood**
The probability that a particular hazard will actually occur.

**ii. Risk Impact**
The severity of the effect that the resulting problem will have on the project.

**iii. Risk Exposure (Risk Value)**
```
Risk Exposure = Risk Likelihood × Risk Impact
```

**Process:**
1. List all identified risks.
2. Score each risk for likelihood (e.g., 1-5 scale).
3. Score each risk for impact (e.g., 1-5 scale).
4. Calculate risk exposure = likelihood × impact.
5. Rank risks by exposure value.
6. Focus management attention on highest-exposure risks.

**Note:** The highest risk value may not be the most likely risk or the one with the highest potential impact individually — it is the combination that matters.

### Q5. Explain risk management strategies.

The following strategies can be used to manage identified risks:

**i. Hazard Prevention**
Prevent the hazard from occurring or reduce its likelihood to insignificant levels.
*Example:* Scheduling key staff meetings well in advance to minimize the risk of unavailability.

**ii. Likelihood Reduction**
Some risks cannot be prevented but their probability can be reduced.
*Example:* User interface prototyping reduces the risk that users will reject the interface.

**iii. Risk Avoidance**
Avoiding activities or technologies that introduce risk.
*Example:* Not using an unproven open-source framework for a critical system.

**iv. Risk Transfer**
Shifting risk responsibility to another party.
*Example:* Outsourcing development of a specialized payment gateway to a certified payment processing company.

**v. Contingency Planning**
Preparing plans to deal with risks that cannot be prevented.
*Example:* Having a backup server configuration plan in case the primary server fails.

### Q6. List different categories of software risks.

Software project risks can be categorized as follows:

**i. Estimation Risks**
Risks arising from inaccurate estimates of size, effort, cost, or schedule.

**ii. Organizational Risks**
Changes in organizational priorities, management changes, or financial difficulties affecting the project.

**iii. People Risks**
Staff unavailability, turnover, lack of skills, poor morale, or communication problems.

**iv. Technical Risks**
New, unproven technology, complex integration requirements, hardware or software failures, or architectural flaws.

**v. Tools Risks**
Unavailability, poor performance, or incompatibility of software development tools.

**vi. Requirements Risks**
Changing or poorly defined requirements, misunderstood client needs.

**vii. External Risks**
Regulatory changes, supplier delays, market shifts, or natural disasters.

### Q7. Differentiate between technical risk and managerial risk.

| Feature | Technical Risk | Managerial Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Risks related to the technology and technical implementation of the project | Risks related to the management processes and organizational factors |
| Examples | New technology failure, hardware incompatibility, complex integration | Poor planning, unclear roles, inadequate communication, budget mismanagement |

### Q8. Explain the importance of risk management in software projects.

Risk management is critically important for the following reasons:

**i. Prevents Project Failure**
Most software project failures can be traced to unmanaged risks. Proactive risk management significantly increases the probability of project success.

**ii. Protects Investment**
Early identification and mitigation of risks protects the financial investment made in the project.

**iii. Enables Better Planning**
Understanding risks leads to more realistic schedules and budgets that include appropriate contingency allowances.

**iv. Improves Decision Making**
Risk information helps project managers and stakeholders make better decisions about project scope, approach, and resource allocation.

**v. Ensures Continuity**
Contingency plans ensure that the project can continue even when unexpected events occur.

**vi. Builds Stakeholder Confidence**
A risk management plan demonstrates professionalism and preparedness to clients and sponsors.

### Q9. Describe the steps involved in risk management framework.

Based on Barry Boehm's Risk Engineering model, the risk management framework includes:

**Step 1: Risk Identification**
List all risks that could adversely affect the project using brainstorming and checklists.

**Step 2: Risk Estimation**
Assess the likelihood and impact of each identified risk.

**Step 3: Risk Evaluation**
Rank risks by calculating risk exposure (likelihood × impact). Determine which risks require priority attention and develop risk aversion strategies.

**Step 4: Risk Planning**
Draw up contingency plans for high-priority risks. Where appropriate, add risk response activities to the project task structure.

**Step 5: Risk Control**
The primary ongoing function — minimize risks and react to problems throughout the project. Includes quality control activities and problem resolution.

**Step 6: Risk Monitoring**
Ongoing activity to track the status of identified risks. As the project progresses, risk likelihood and impact can change and must be re-assessed.

**Step 7: Risk Directing and Staffing**
Day-to-day management of risk. Involves directing team members on risk responses and planning for additional staff if risk mitigation requires it.

### Q10. What are contingency plans in risk management?

Contingency plans are pre-prepared responses to specific risks that are activated only if and when the corresponding risk event actually occurs.

**Key Characteristics:**
- They are developed during the risk planning phase.
- They define specific actions to be taken when a risk materializes.
- They help minimize the impact of risks that could not be avoided.
- They are integrated into the project plan as contingency tasks.

**Examples of Contingency Plans:**

**i. Staff Absence**
Risk: A key developer becomes ill.
Contingency Plan: Cross-train at least one other team member on the critical modules so they can take over.

**ii. Technology Failure**
Risk: The primary development server crashes.
Contingency Plan: Maintain regular backups and have an alternative server configured and ready to deploy.

**iii. Supplier Delay**
Risk: A third-party API provider delays their service delivery.
Contingency Plan: Identify an alternative provider in advance and maintain integration documentation for both.

**iv. Changing Requirements**
Risk: Client requests significant feature changes mid-project.
Contingency Plan: Formal change control process with impact assessment and schedule/budget renegotiation procedures.
